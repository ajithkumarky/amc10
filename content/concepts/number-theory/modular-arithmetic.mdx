## Overview

We write $a \equiv b \pmod{n}$ to mean "$a$ and $b$ have the same remainder when divided by $n$," or equivalently, $n$ divides $a - b$. For example, $17 \equiv 2 \pmod{5}$ because $17 - 2 = 15 = 3 \cdot 5$.

Modular arithmetic turns problems about huge numbers into problems about small residues. Instead of computing $7^{2024}$ exactly, you only track what it looks like mod 10, shrinking billions of digits down to a single digit.

AMC 10 problems love modular arithmetic because it appears in at least three guises: **units-digit questions** (work mod 10), **remainder questions** (work mod the given divisor), and **divisibility tests** (mod 9 for digit sums, mod 4 for last two digits, etc.). Recognizing which modulus to use is often the key step.

## Key facts

- **Congruence rule:** $a \equiv b \pmod{n}$ if and only if $n \mid (a - b)$.
- **Addition and multiplication:** If $a \equiv b$ and $c \equiv d \pmod{n}$, then $a + c \equiv b + d$ and $a \cdot c \equiv b \cdot d \pmod{n}$. You can reduce before multiplying.
- **Powers cycle:** The residues of $a^1, a^2, a^3, \ldots$ mod $n$ eventually repeat. Find the period and reduce the exponent by that period.  
  Common cycles mod 10 (units digits):  
  | base mod 10 | cycle | period |
  |------------|-------|--------|
  | 1 | 1 | 1 |
  | 2 | 2, 4, 8, 6 | 4 |
  | 3 | 3, 9, 7, 1 | 4 |
  | 4 | 4, 6 | 2 |
  | 5 | 5 | 1 |
  | 6 | 6 | 1 |
  | 7 | 7, 9, 3, 1 | 4 |
  | 8 | 8, 4, 2, 6 | 4 |
  | 9 | 9, 1 | 2 |
- **Euler's theorem:** If $\gcd(a, n) = 1$ then $a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod{n}$, where $\phi(n)$ is Euler's totient. For $n = p$ prime, this reduces to Fermat's little theorem: $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$.
- **Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT) — light version:** If $m$ and $n$ are coprime, then knowing $x \pmod{m}$ and $x \pmod{n}$ uniquely determines $x \pmod{mn}$. Use this when a problem gives two separate remainder conditions.
- **Division warning:** You can divide both sides by $k$ only if $\gcd(k, n) = 1$. Dividing $6 \equiv 0 \pmod{6}$ by 2 gives $3 \equiv 0 \pmod{6}$, which is false.

## Worked example 1

> What is the units digit of $7^{2025}$?

Work mod 10. The powers of 7 cycle through units digits $7, 9, 3, 1$ with period 4.

Divide the exponent: $2025 = 4 \cdot 506 + 1$, so $2025 \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$.

Therefore $7^{2025}$ has the same units digit as $7^1$, which is $\mathbf{7}$.

## Worked example 2

> Find the remainder when $5^{40} + 3^{40}$ is divided by 8.

Work mod 8. Note $5 \equiv 5 \pmod{8}$. Compute the cycle of $5^k \pmod{8}$:
$$5^1 \equiv 5,\quad 5^2 = 25 \equiv 1,\quad 5^3 \equiv 5,\ldots$$
The cycle has period 2. Since 40 is even, $5^{40} \equiv 1 \pmod{8}$.

Now for $3^k \pmod{8}$:
$$3^1 \equiv 3,\quad 3^2 = 9 \equiv 1,\quad 3^3 \equiv 3,\ldots$$
Period 2 again. Since 40 is even, $3^{40} \equiv 1 \pmod{8}$.

So $5^{40} + 3^{40} \equiv 1 + 1 = 2 \pmod{8}$. The remainder is $\mathbf{2}$.

## Common traps

- **Forgetting to reduce the base first.** Always reduce each factor mod $n$ before multiplying; e.g., to work with $97 \cdot 103 \pmod{10}$, use $7 \cdot 3 = 21 \equiv 1$.
- **Off-by-one in cycle position.** When the exponent is a multiple of the period, the answer is the *last* element of the cycle, not the first. E.g., $7^4 \equiv 1 \pmod{10}$, not 7.
- **Dividing without checking $\gcd$.** You cannot cancel a common factor unless it is coprime to the modulus.
- **Mixing up "remainder" and "residue".** Remainders are always non-negative. If you compute $a \equiv -1 \pmod{n}$, the remainder is $n - 1$, not $-1$.
- **Applying CRT when moduli share a factor.** CRT requires the moduli to be pairwise coprime. If they are not, check consistency and adjust.
