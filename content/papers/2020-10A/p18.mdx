<Problem>
Let $(a, b, c, d)$ be an ordered quadruple of not necessarily distinct integers, each one of them in the set $\{0, 1, 2, 3\}$. For how many such quadruples is it true that $a \cdot d - b \cdot c$ is odd? (For example, $(0, 3, 1, 1)$ is one such quadruple, because $0 \cdot 1 - 3 \cdot 1 = -3$ is odd.)
</Problem>

<Solution>
For $ad - bc$ to be odd, the two terms $ad$ and $bc$ must have different parities — one is even and one is odd. A product of two integers is odd if and only if both integers are odd.

In $\{0, 1, 2, 3\}$: the odd integers are $1$ and $3$ (2 choices), the even integers are $0$ and $2$ (2 choices). Each element is even with probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and odd with probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$.

**Case 1: $ad$ is odd and $bc$ is even.**

- $ad$ odd: $a$ odd AND $d$ odd — $2 \times 2 = 4$ ways.
- $bc$ even: at least one of $b, c$ is even. Total pairs $(b,c)$: $4^2 = 16$. Both odd: $2 \times 2 = 4$ ways. So even product: $16 - 4 = 12$ ways.
- Subtotal: $4 \times 12 = 48$.

**Case 2: $ad$ is even and $bc$ is odd.**

- $ad$ even: $16 - 4 = 12$ ways.
- $bc$ odd: $4$ ways.
- Subtotal: $12 \times 4 = 48$.

**Total:** $48 + 48 = 96$.

Answer: **C**.
</Solution>
