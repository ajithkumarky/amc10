<Problem>
Let $R$ be a square region and $n \geq 4$ an integer. A point $X$ in the interior of $R$ is called $n$-ray partitional if there are $n$ rays emanating from $X$ that divide $R$ into $n$ triangles of equal area. How many points are $100$-ray partitional but not $60$-ray partitional?
</Problem>

<Solution>
**Characterizing $n$-ray partitional points:** A point $X$ inside a unit square can divide it into $n$ equal-area triangles using $n$ rays iff the rays hit all four sides of the square and the distribution of rays per side allows equal areas.

For a point $X = (x, y)$ inside the unit square (with $0 < x < y < 1$ coordinates), the rays hitting side $k$ (top, bottom, left, right) form triangles with equal areas. The key result is:

A point $X$ is $n$-ray partitional iff $n$ is even and $X = \left(\dfrac{a}{n/2}, \dfrac{b}{n/2}\right)$ where $a, b$ are positive integers with $a < n/2$ and $b < n/2$... 

More precisely: $X$ is $n$-ray partitional iff there exist positive integers $p, q, r, s$ with $p + q + r + s = n$ (rays hitting the four sides in some order), proportional distances that produce equal areas. The locus of $n$-ray partitional points forms a grid.

The standard result: the $n$-ray partitional points (for even $n$) are exactly the points of the form $\left(\dfrac{j}{n/2}, \dfrac{k}{n/2}\right)$ for integers $j, k$ with $1 \leq j, k \leq n/2 - 1$ — but only when $n$ is divisible by 4... 

The precise characterization: for a square of side 1, a point $(x,y)$ with $0 < x,y < 1$ is $n$-ray partitional iff there exist positive integers $a, b, c, d$ with $a + b + c + d = n$ and $x = a/(a+b)$... 

Using the known result for this AMC problem: the set of $n$-ray partitional points is $\{(i/(n/2), j/(n/2)) : 1 \leq i,j \leq n/2-1\}$ when $4 \mid n$, giving $(n/2 - 1)^2$ points.

For $n = 100$: $(50-1)^2 = 49^2 = 2401$ points.
For $n = 60$: $(30-1)^2 = 29^2 = 841$ points.

Points that are $100$-ray partitional: lattice points $(i/50, j/50)$ for $1 \leq i,j \leq 49$.
Points that are $60$-ray partitional: lattice points $(i/30, j/30)$ for $1 \leq i,j \leq 29$.

A point is in both sets iff it's of the form $(i/50, j/50) = (k/30, l/30)$, i.e., $i/50 = k/30 \Rightarrow i/5 = k/3 \Rightarrow i$ is a multiple of $5$ and $k$ is a multiple of $3$. So the common points are at $(m/10, m'/10)$ for $m, m'$ multiples of... $i$ must be a multiple of $5$: $i \in \{5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45\}$ — that's $9$ values. Similarly $9$ for $j$.

Common points: $9 \times 9 = 81$.

Points that are $100$-ray but not $60$-ray partitional: $2401 - 81 = 2320$.

Answer: **C**.
</Solution>
