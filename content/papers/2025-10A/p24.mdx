<Problem>
Call a positive integer *fair* if no digit is used more than once, it has no $0$s, and no digit is adjacent to two greater digits. For example, $196$, $23$, and $12463$ are fair, but $1546$, $320$, and $34321$ are not. How many fair positive integers are there?
</Problem>

<Solution>
A fair integer uses digits from $\{1, 2, \ldots, 9\}$ with no repeats. The condition "no digit is adjacent to two greater digits" means: no interior digit is a strict local minimum (i.e., no interior digit has both neighbors larger than itself).

**Key observation.** In a sequence with no interior local minimum, the sequence must be "unimodal" — it goes up (weakly or not at all) and then down (weakly), possibly with a plateau at the peak. More precisely, the digits form a pattern that increases (non-strictly) from the left up to some peak, then decreases (non-strictly). But since all digits are distinct, the pattern is: strictly increasing up to a peak, then strictly decreasing.

Such a sequence is called a **unimodal permutation** of the chosen digit set.

**Counting.** Choose any non-empty subset $S$ of $\{1,2,\ldots,9\}$ with $|S| = k$. Given $S$, how many fair integers can be formed using exactly the digits of $S$?

For a fair arrangement of $k$ distinct digits: the sequence must be unimodal (increase then decrease). The maximum element of $S$ must be the peak. We need to split the remaining $k-1$ elements into a left-increasing part and a right-decreasing part (both written in order).

For each of the $k-1$ non-maximum elements of $S$, it either goes to the left of the peak or to the right of the peak. Once we decide which side, the order is determined (left side: increasing order; right side: decreasing order). The left side can be empty (peak is first) and the right side can be empty (peak is last), as long as the whole number makes sense.

So there are $2^{k-1}$ unimodal arrangements of a $k$-element set.

**Total fair integers:**
$$\sum_{k=1}^{9} \binom{9}{k} \cdot 2^{k-1} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{9}\binom{9}{k}2^k = \frac{1}{2}\left[(1+2)^9 - \binom{9}{0}\cdot 1\right] = \frac{3^9 - 1}{2} = \frac{19683 - 1}{2} = \frac{19682}{2} = 9841.$$

**Verification with small examples.** $196$: digits $1, 9, 6$. Peak is $9$; left side $\{1\}$ gives $1$; right side $\{6\}$ gives $6$. Sequence $1,9,6$ ✓. $34321$ has $4$ in the interior with neighbors $3$ and $3$ — neither is greater, so the issue is $3, 4, 3, 2, 1$: the digit $4$ has neighbors $3$ and $3$, that's fine. The digit $3$ (second from left) has neighbors $4$ and $3$, and $4 > 3$ but $3 \not> 3$, so OK. Actually $34321$: reading as $3,4,3,2,1$ — digit $3$ appears twice, violating the "no repeats" rule, so $34321$ fails on repetition.

Answer: **C**.
</Solution>
