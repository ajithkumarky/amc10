<Problem>
For a set of four distinct lines in a plane, there are exactly $N$ distinct points that lie on two or more of the lines. What is the sum of all possible values of $N$?
</Problem>

<Solution>
Four distinct lines in a plane can intersect in at most $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ points (one per pair). The actual number of intersection points depends on how many pairs of lines are parallel or concurrent (share an intersection point).

We enumerate all possible values of $N$ by considering the configurations:

**No two lines parallel, no three concurrent:** All $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ pairs give distinct intersection points. $N = 6$.

**Exactly one pair of parallel lines, no three concurrent:** That pair gives 0 intersections; the remaining $\binom{4}{2} - 1 = 5$ pairs each give a distinct point (assuming no three concurrent). $N = 5$.

**Exactly two pairs of parallel lines (two sets of two parallel lines):** Two pairs contribute 0 intersections each; the remaining $4$ pairs give points. If no three are concurrent, $N = 4$.

**Three lines parallel, the fourth not:** The three parallel lines contribute 0 pairwise intersections. The fourth line intersects each of the three parallel lines in 3 distinct points. $N = 3$.

**All four lines parallel:** No intersections. $N = 0$.

**Three lines concurrent (meet at one point), fourth line not parallel to the others and not through that point:** The three concurrent lines contribute 1 intersection point (instead of 3). The fourth line intersects each of the three lines in 3 distinct new points. Total: $1 + 3 = 4$. But wait, these 4 points are distinct (the point of concurrence and 3 new ones). $N = 4$. (Same value as above.)

**Three lines concurrent, fourth line through the concurrent point:** All four lines pass through one point. $N = 1$.

**Two pairs of parallel lines but wait — let me be more systematic.**

Actually, let me list all distinct values:

- $N = 0$: all four lines parallel.
- $N = 1$: all four concurrent.
- $N = 3$: three parallel lines plus one transversal.
- $N = 4$: two parallel pairs (giving $2 \times 2 = 4$ intersections from the cross-pairs), OR three concurrent lines plus a fourth giving $1 + 3 = 4$.
- $N = 5$: one parallel pair plus two others in general position (check: 0 + 5 distinct points = 5). OR: three concurrent, one parallel to none and not through the point — gives $1 + 3 = 4$ (covered above).
- $N = 6$: general position (no parallel lines, no three concurrent).

Can $N = 2$? For $N = 2$, we'd need exactly 2 intersection points. With four lines, having only 2 intersection points requires heavy concurrences or parallels. Two concurrent pairs: lines $\{a,b\}$ meet at $P$ and lines $\{c,d\}$ meet at $Q$, with $a \parallel c$, $a \parallel d$... this quickly forces contradictions. Actually: two groups of two concurrent lines where lines between groups are parallel — e.g., $a \parallel c$, $a \parallel d$, $b \parallel c$, $b \parallel d$ — but then $a \parallel b$, reducing to the case of all four parallel. Alternatively, two pairs of parallel lines (two parallel, two parallel) give $N = 4$ as computed. It turns out $N = 2$ is not achievable.

The possible values are $N \in \{0, 1, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$.

Sum $= 0 + 1 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 = 19$.

Answer: **D**.
</Solution>
