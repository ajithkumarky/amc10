<Problem>
What is the largest integer that is a divisor of
$$(n+1)(n+3)(n+5)(n+7)(n+9)$$
for all positive even integers $n$?
</Problem>

<Solution>
When $n$ is a positive even integer, each of $n+1, n+3, n+5, n+7, n+9$ is odd. These five numbers form a sequence of five consecutive odd integers.

**Divisibility by 3:** Among any three consecutive odd integers, exactly one is divisible by $3$. Among five consecutive odd integers, at least one (in fact, at least one and possibly two) must be divisible by $3$. So the product is always divisible by $3$.

**Divisibility by 5:** Among any five consecutive odd integers, exactly one is divisible by $5$. So the product is always divisible by $5$.

**Divisibility by 15:** Both conditions hold simultaneously, so $15 \mid (n+1)(n+3)(n+5)(n+7)(n+9)$ for all positive even $n$.

**Check $n = 2$:** $(3)(5)(7)(9)(11) = 10395 = 15 \times 693$. ✓

**Why not $165 = 3 \times 5 \times 11$?** For $n = 2$: $10395 / 11 = 945$, which is divisible by $11$. For $n = 4$: $(5)(7)(9)(11)(13) = 45045$. Is $45045$ divisible by $11$? $45045 / 11 = 4095$. Yes. For $n = 20$: $(21)(23)(25)(27)(29) = 21 \times 23 \times 25 \times 27 \times 29$. None of these is divisible by $11$ (checking: $21 = 3\times7$, $23$ prime, $25=5^2$, $27=3^3$, $29$ prime). So $11 \nmid$ the product when $n=20$.

Therefore the largest guaranteed divisor is $15$.

Answer: **D**.
</Solution>
