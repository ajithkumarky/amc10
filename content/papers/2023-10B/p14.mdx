<Problem>
How many ordered pairs of integers $(m, n)$ satisfy the equation $m^2 + mn + n^2 = m^2 n^2$?
</Problem>

<Solution>
We look for all integer solutions to $m^2 + mn + n^2 = m^2 n^2$.

**Case 1: $m = 0$ or $n = 0$.**

If $m = 0$: the equation becomes $n^2 = 0$, so $n = 0$. This gives the solution $(0, 0)$.

If $n = 0$: symmetrically, $m = 0$, giving the same solution.

**Case 2: Both $m \neq 0$ and $n \neq 0$.**

Rewrite as $m^2 n^2 - m^2 - mn - n^2 = 0$. Divide through by $m^2 n^2$ (valid since both are nonzero):
$$1 - \frac{1}{n^2} - \frac{1}{mn} - \frac{1}{m^2} = 0.$$

Let $a = \tfrac{1}{m}$ and $b = \tfrac{1}{n}$ (rational numbers). Then $1 - b^2 - ab - a^2 = 0$, i.e., $a^2 + ab + b^2 = 1$.

Since $m$ and $n$ are nonzero integers, $|a| \leq 1$ and $|b| \leq 1$, so $|a|, |b| \in \{1\}$ (only $m, n = \pm 1$ give $|a| = 1$).

Try $(m, n) = (1, 1)$: $1 + 1 + 1 = 3 \neq 1$. No.
Try $(m, n) = (1, -1)$: $1 - 1 + 1 = 1$ and $m^2 n^2 = 1$. Yes!
Try $(m, n) = (-1, 1)$: $1 - 1 + 1 = 1 = 1$. Yes!
Try $(m, n) = (-1, -1)$: $1 + 1 + 1 = 3 \neq 1$. No.

For $|m| \geq 2$ or $|n| \geq 2$, we can show no solutions exist. If both $|m|, |n| \geq 2$, then $m^2 n^2 \geq 4\max(m^2, n^2) > m^2 + |mn| + n^2$ since $4\max(m^2,n^2) \geq 4m^2/2 + 4n^2/2 > m^2+n^2+|mn|$ for $|m|,|n|\geq 2$. If exactly one of $|m|=1$, say $m=\pm 1$, then $m^2=1$ and the equation becomes $1 + n + n^2 = n^2$ (if $m=1$), giving $n = -1$ (already found), or $1 - n + n^2 = n^2$ (if $m=-1$), giving $n = 1$ (already found).

**Summary:** The three solutions are $(0, 0)$, $(1, -1)$, and $(-1, 1)$.

Answer: **C**.
</Solution>
