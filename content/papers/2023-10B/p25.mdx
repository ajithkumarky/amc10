<Problem>
A regular pentagon with area $1 + \sqrt{5}$ is printed on paper and cut out. All five vertices are folded to the center of the pentagon, creating a smaller pentagon. What is the area of the new pentagon?
</Problem>

<Solution>
**Setting up coordinates.** Let the original regular pentagon have circumradius $R$ (center-to-vertex distance). Its area is
$$A_{\text{orig}} = \frac{5}{2}R^2 \sin 72° = 1 + \sqrt{5}.$$

**What folding a vertex does.** When vertex $V$ is folded to the center $O$, the fold line is the perpendicular bisector of segment $VO$, located at distance $R/2$ from $O$. The triangular flap of the pentagon beyond this fold line (the region closer to $V$ than to $O$) gets folded inward.

**Identifying the new pentagon.** The five fold lines are each at distance $R/2$ from the center, perpendicular to the five radii. These five lines form a smaller regular pentagon whose **apothem** (center-to-side distance) is $R/2$.

For a regular pentagon with apothem $r$, the circumradius is $R' = r / \cos 36°$ and the area is
$$A = \frac{5r^2}{\tan 36°} = 5r^2 \cot 36°.$$

Alternatively, the area scales as the square of any linear dimension. The original pentagon has apothem $r_{\text{orig}} = R \cos 36°$. The new pentagon has apothem $r_{\text{new}} = R/2$.

**Area ratio:**
$$\frac{A_{\text{new}}}{A_{\text{orig}}} = \left(\frac{r_{\text{new}}}{r_{\text{orig}}}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{R/2}{R\cos 36°}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{4\cos^2 36°}.$$

**Using exact values.** We know $\cos 36° = \dfrac{1+\sqrt{5}}{4}$... let's be precise:

$$\cos 36° = \frac{\sqrt{5}+1}{4}.$$

Wait — actually $\cos 36° = \dfrac{\sqrt{5}+1}{4}$ is incorrect. The exact value is $\cos 36° = \dfrac{1+\sqrt{5}}{4}$... Let me use $\cos 36° = \dfrac{\sqrt{5}+1}{4}$. No — the standard exact value is:
$$\cos 36° = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{4}.$$

This gives $\cos^2 36° = \dfrac{(1+\sqrt{5})^2}{16} = \dfrac{6+2\sqrt{5}}{16} = \dfrac{3+\sqrt{5}}{8}$.

$$A_{\text{new}} = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{4\cdot\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{8}} = \frac{(1+\sqrt{5})\cdot 8}{4(3+\sqrt{5})} = \frac{2(1+\sqrt{5})}{3+\sqrt{5}}.$$

Rationalize by multiplying by $\dfrac{3-\sqrt{5}}{3-\sqrt{5}}$:
$$= \frac{2(1+\sqrt{5})(3-\sqrt{5})}{(3)^2-(\sqrt{5})^2} = \frac{2(3-\sqrt{5}+3\sqrt{5}-5)}{9-5} = \frac{2(2\sqrt{5}-2)}{4} = \frac{4(\sqrt{5}-1)}{4} = \sqrt{5}-1.$$

Answer: **B**.
</Solution>
